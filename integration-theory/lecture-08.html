8  Product Measures

Our goal of this new chapter is to, given two measure spaces, define a product of them. For the product space, we should obviously use (\(X\times Y\)). For the \(\sigma\)-algebra, it should be the smallest which contains the so-called “rectangles” \[\mathcal R = \{A \times B : A \in \mathcal M, B \in \mathcal N\}\]whose \(\sigma\)-algebra \(\mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N \equiv \sigma(\mathcal R)\) we denote as \(\mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N\). It is clear that if we did have a product measure, then it should satisfy \[(\mu \times \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A) \nu(B) \qquad \forall A \in \mathcal M, B \in \mathcal N\]

ImportantTheorem 8.1 — Existence of product measures

There exists a measure \(\mu \times \nu\) on the measurable space \((X\times Y, \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N)\) such that

\[ (\mu \times \nu)(A \times B) = \mu(A)\nu(B). \]

Moreover, \(\mu\times\nu\) is the unique measure satisfying these properties if both \(\mu,\nu\) are \(\sigma\)-finite.

NoteDefinition 8.2 — Sections

If \(E\subseteq X \times Y\), the \(x\)-section of \(E\) is

\[E_x := \{y\in Y : (x,y) \in E\}\]

and the \(y\)-section of \(E\) is

\[E^y := \{x \in X : (x,y) \in E\}\]

NoteDefinition 8.3 — Sections of functions

If \(f : X \times Y \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\), we let for a fixed \(x\in X\)

\[f_x: Y \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \quad \text{given by} \quad f_x(y) := f(x,y)\]

and for a fixed \(y\in Y\)

\[f^y: X \rightarrow \mathbb{R} \quad \text{given by} \quad f^y(x) := f(x,y)\]

ImportantProposition 8.4 — Sections of measurable sets are measurable and sections of functions are measurable

(i) If \(E \in \mathcal{M} \otimes \mathcal{N}\) then for all \(x\in X\), \[E_x \in \mathcal{N}\] and for all \(y\in Y\), \[E^y \in \mathcal{M}\]

(ii) If \(f: X \times Y \rightarrow \mathbb{R}\) is \(\mathcal{M}\otimes \mathcal{N}\)-measurable, then for all \(x\in X\), \(f_x\) is \(\mathcal{N}\)-measurable and for all \(y \in Y\), \(f^y\) is \(\mathcal{M}\)-measurable.

Fubini’s theorem for sets

ImportantTheorem 8.5 — Fubini’s theorem for sets

Let \((X,\mathcal M, \mu)\) and \((Y,\mathcal N, \nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces. If \(E\in\mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N\), then the maps \(x\rightarrow \nu(E_x)\) and \(y\rightarrow \mu(E^y)\) are measurable functions on \(X\) and \(Y\) respectively, furthermore

\[(\mu \times \nu)(E) = \int_X \nu(E_x) d\mu(x) = \int_Y \mu(E^y) d\nu(y)\]

NoteProof

We first prove it where we assume the spaces are finite. By symmetry, it is enough to show that \(x\rightarrow \nu(E_x)\) is measurable and \((\mu\times \nu)(E) = \int_X \nu(E_x) d\mu(x)\). Let \[\mathcal C := \{E \in \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N : \text{the above claim holds}\}\]. Our goal is to show that \(\mathcal C = \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N\). Note that \(\mathcal C \subseteq \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N\) trivially. We are therefore left with proving the reverse containment.

Step 1 \(\mathcal R \subseteq \mathcal C\). \(\nu((A\times B)_x) = \nu(B)1_A(x)\) hence \(x\rightarrow \nu((A\times B)_x)\) is measurable (constant times a measurable function). In addition \[(\mu \times \nu)(A \times B) = \int_X 1_A(x) \nu(B) d\mu(x) = \int_X \nu((A\times B)_x) d\mu(x)\]

Step 2: \(\mathcal C\) is a \(\mathcal D\)-system. We check each axiom.

  1. \(X\times Y\in\mathcal C\). \((X\times Y)_x = Y\) for all \(x\), so \(x\mapsto\nu(Y)\) is constant, hence measurable (M). For (I): \((\mu\times\nu)(X\times Y) = \mu(X)\nu(Y) = \int_X\nu(Y)\,d\mu(x)\).

  2. Closed under proper differences. Let \(E\subseteq F\) with \(E,F\in\mathcal C\). Then \((F\setminus E)_x = F_x\setminus E_x\) and \(E_x\subseteq F_x\), so \(\nu((F\setminus E)_x) = \nu(F_x)-\nu(E_x)\), valid since finiteness gives \(\nu(F_x)<\infty\). Both maps are measurable by assumption (\(E,F\in\mathcal C\)), so their difference is measurable (M). For (I): \[\int_X\nu((F\setminus E)_x)\,d\mu = \int_X\nu(F_x)\,d\mu - \int_X\nu(E_x)\,d\mu = (\mu\times\nu)(F) - (\mu\times\nu)(E) = (\mu\times\nu)(F\setminus E)\]

  3. Closed under increasing unions. Let \(E_n\nearrow E\) with each \(E_n\in\mathcal C\). Then \((E_n)_x\nearrow E_x\) for every \(x\), so by continuity from below \(\nu((E_n)_x)\nearrow\nu(E_x)\). Each \(x\mapsto\nu((E_n)_x)\) is measurable by assumption, and a pointwise limit of measurable functions is measurable by Proposition 5.6 in Lecture 5 (M). For (I), apply MCT (the functions are nonneg, measurable, and increasing): \[\int_X\nu(E_x)\,d\mu = \int_X\lim_n\nu((E_n)_x)\,d\mu = \lim_n\int_X\nu((E_n)_x)\,d\mu = \lim_n(\mu\times\nu)(E_n) = (\mu\times\nu)(E)\] > > where the last equality is continuity from below of \(\mu\times\nu\).

Step 3 Since \(\mathcal R\) is a \(\pi\)-system, the Dynkin \(\pi\)-\(\lambda\) theorem gives \(\mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N = \sigma(\mathcal R) = \mathcal D(\mathcal R)\subseteq C\). The last inclusion follows from the fact that \(\mathcal R \subseteq C\) and \(\mathcal C\) is a \(\mathcal D\)-system, where \(\mathcal D(\mathcal R)\) is the smallest \(\mathcal D\)-system generated by \(\mathcal R\). Thus we have that the statement holds for the finite case.

Step 4. Moving to the \(\sigma\)-finite case, let \(X_1\subseteq X_2\ldots\) have finite measure with \(X=\bigcup X_i\) and \(Y_1\subseteq Y_2\ldots\) with \(Y = \bigcup Y_i\). NTS: Note that this is different from the sigma-finite definition where we do not demand an increasing sequence of subsets, however, given any exhaustion \(\{A_n\}\) we can simply replace it with \(X_n:= \bigcup_{i=1}^n A_i\). Consider the measure \((\mu \times \nu)\vert_{X_n \times Y_n}\) which is a finite measure on \((X \times Y, \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N)\). It is easy to see that \((\mu \times \nu)\vert_{X_n \times Y_n} = \mu \vert_{X_n} \times \nu \vert_{Y_n}\) (this follow from the fact that \(\mu \vert_{X_n}(A) = \mu(X_n \cap A) \leq \mu(X_n) < \infty\), and thus we have a product of finite measures which is finite). By the finite case, for each \(E \in \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N\), the map \(x\rightarrow \nu \vert_{Y_n}(E_x) = \nu(Y_n\cap E_x)\) is measurable on \((X, \mathcal M)\) and \[\mu\times\nu((X_n\times Y_n)\cap E) = (\mu\times\nu)|_{X_n\times Y_n}(E) = \int_X \nu|_{Y_n}(E_x)\,d\mu|_{X_n}(x) = \int_X \mathbf{1}_{X_n}\nu(Y_n\cap E_x)\,d\mu(x)\] Using continuity from below and letting \(n\to\infty\), we conclude that \(x\to\nu(E_x)\) is measurable on \((X,\mathcal{M})\). Also, letting \(n\to\infty\) in the above display, using continuity from below, the fact that product of two measurable functions is measurable, and the MCT, we conclude that \[(\mu\times\nu)(E) = \int_X \nu(E_x)\,d\mu(x)\] as desired. \(\square\)

Note that \(x\rightarrow \nu(E_X)\) is a map from \(X\rightarrow [0,\infty]\) since \(E_x\) is a subset of \(Y\) and \(\nu(E_x) \in [0,\infty]\).

Counterexample without \(\sigma\)-finiteness

Consider two measurable spaces on \([0,1]\) with the Borel sets with the first endowed with a measure \(\mu\) being the Lebesgue measure and the second endowed with a measure \(\nu\) being the counting measure (the latter not being \(\sigma\)-finite). Let \(\mathcal D:= \{(x,x) : x\in[0,1]\}\) be the diagonal. We first check that \(\mathcal D\) lies in the product \(\sigma\)-algebra. The complement \(\mathcal D^c\) is open in \([0,1]^2\) (open balls are taken relative to the square). For any point \((x,y) \in \mathcal D^c\) there is an \(\epsilon>0\) such that the open ball of radius \(\epsilon\) around \((x,y)\) lies in \(\mathcal D^c\), so we can find rationals \(a,b,c,d\in\mathbb Q\) with \((x,y) \in (a,b) \times (c,d) \subseteq \mathcal D^c\). The collection \(\mathcal B\) of all such rectangles is countable, since it is indexed by \(\mathbb Q^4\), and \[\mathcal D^c = \bigcup \{B \in \mathcal B : B \subseteq \mathcal D^c\}.\] Each open interval is a Borel set, so each \(B\) is a measurable rectangle. The product \(\sigma\)-algebra is closed under countable unions and complements, so \(\mathcal D^c\), and hence \(\mathcal D\), belongs to it. Note that \(\mathcal D_x = \{x\}\) for each \(x\in X\) and \(\mathcal D^y = \{y\}\) for each \(y\in Y\). The map \(x\rightarrow \nu(\mathcal D_x)\) is the constant function 1 (which is measurable) whereas \(y\rightarrow \mu(\mathcal D^y)\) is the constant function 0 (which is measurable). However, we now have \[\int_X \nu(\mathcal D_x)\, d\mu(x) = 1 \neq 0 = \int_Y \mu(\mathcal D^y)\, d\nu(y).\]

The Fubini and Tonelli theorems

Theorem 8.5 was a special case of a more general theorem which we now present.

ImportantTheorem 8.6 — Tonelli and Fubini

Let \((X,\mathcal M, \mu)\) and \((Y, \mathcal N, \nu)\) be \(\sigma\)-finite measure spaces.

(a) (Tonelli) If \(f\in L^+(X\times Y, \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N, \mu \times \nu)\) then \[g(x) := \int_Y f_x(y) d\nu(y) \in L^+(X, \mathcal M, \mu)\] and \[\int_{X \times Y} f(x,y) d(\mu \times \nu)(x,y) = \int_X g(x) d\mu(x)\]

(b) (Fubini) If \(f\in L^1(X\times Y, \mathcal M \otimes \mathcal N, \mu \times \nu)\) then \(f_x \in L^1(Y, \mathcal N, \nu)\) for \(\mu\)-a.e. \(x\in X\). Furthermore, \[g(x) = \int_Y f_x(y) d\nu(y) \in L^1(X, \mathcal M, \mu)\] which is defined \(\mu\)-a.e. x, belongs to \(L^1(X, \mathcal M, \mu)\) and \[\int_{X\times Y} f(x,y) d(\mu \times \nu)(x,y) = \int_X g(x) d\mu(x)\]

Note that for Tonelli, we assume \(f\in L^+\), i.e. \(f\geq 0\) and measurable in the product space. It thus follows from Proposition 8.4 that each section \(f_x\) is \(\mathcal N\)-measurable, and since \(f_x \geq 0\) (from \(f \geq 0\)), we also have \(f_x \in L^+(Y,\mathcal N, \nu)\) for free.

In the Tonelli case, there is no integrability requirement, i.e the double integral could be infinite, but the iterated integral formula holds unconditionally. In the Fubini case, \(f\) is Lebesgue integrable, (remember that \(f\in L^1\) implies that it is Lebesgue integrable. See Lecture 6).

Finally, note the consequence that \(f_x \in L^1\). This follows by applying Tonelli to \(|f| \geq 0\) meaning \[\int_{X\times Y} |f| d(\mu\times \nu) = \int_X \int_Y |f_x| d\nu d\mu(x) < \infty\]since \(f\in L^1\) and thus \(\int_Y |f_x| d\nu := h(x)\) must be finite \(\mu\)-a.e., meaning that \(f_x \in L^1\) \(\mu\)-a.e..

Fubini from Tonelli.

Take \(f\in L^1\), so \(f = f^+ - f^-\) with both \(f^+, f^-\in L^+\). Apply Tonelli to each separately:

\[\int_{X\times Y} f^+\, d(\mu\times\nu) = \int_X\left(\int_Y f^+_x\, d\nu\right)d\mu\]

\[\int_{X\times Y} f^-\, d(\mu\times\nu) = \int_X\left(\int_Y f^-_x\, d\nu\right)d\mu\]

Since \(f\in L^1\), both sides are finite, so we can subtract:

\[\int_{X\times Y} f\, d(\mu\times\nu) = \int_X\left(\int_Y f^+_x\, d\nu - \int_Y f^-_x\, d\nu\right)d\mu = \int_X\left(\int_Y f_x\, d\nu\right)d\mu\]

The subtraction inside the inner integral is valid for \(\mu\)-a.e. \(x\) — specifically those \(x\) where \(\int_Y f^-_x\,d\nu < \infty\) (i.e. it is fine to be infinite on nullsets).

The \(L^1\) assumption is stronger than \(L^+\), which is why Fubini gives more, not only can we swap integrals, but also that the sections are integrable a.e.
