4  The Legendre Transformation

In Chapter 3 we defined the Hamiltonian. It is obtained by computing the conjugate momenta \(p_\mu = \partial L/\partial \dot q^\mu\) and performing a Legendre transformation, \[H(q^\mu, p_\mu, t) = p_\mu \dot q^\mu - L.\]

A one-dimensional example of the Legendre transform

The Legendre transform has a simple geometric interpretation in one dimension (Figure 4.1). Let \(f\) be strictly convex (or strictly concave) and fix a point \(x_0\). The tangent line to \(f\) there has slope \(p = f'(x_0)\), so in point-slope form it reads \[y = f(x_0) + p\,(x - x_0) = p\,x - \bigl[\,p\,x_0 - f(x_0)\,\bigr].\] We name the bracket \(g(p) := p\,x_0 - f(x_0)\), so that \(-g(p)\) is where the tangent line crosses the vertical axis. Since \(f\) is strictly convex, the slope \(p\) determines the point \(x_0\) uniquely, \(x_0 = x(p)\). Then \(g\) becomes a function of \(p\) alone, \[g(p) = p\, x(p) - f\bigl(x(p)\bigr), \tag{4.1}\] and \(g\) is called the Legendre transform of \(f\).

To see what \(g\) looks like, drag the tangent point in Figure 4.2: each tangent line of \(f\) on the left becomes a single point \(\bigl(p, g(p)\bigr)\) on the right.1

1 Note that \(x^2/2\) is its own Legendre transform.

The Hamiltonian as a Legendre transform

The Hamiltonian is the Legendre transform of \(L\) as a function of the velocities \(\dot q^\mu\) (with \(q^\mu\) and \(t\) held fixed), the slopes being the momenta \(p_\mu = \partial L/\partial \dot q^\mu\). For a more detailed treatment, see Cline [1].

When can we perform the Legendre transformation?

To build \(H(q^\mu, p_\mu, t)\) we must solve \(p_\mu = \partial L/\partial \dot q^\mu\) for the velocities \(\dot q^\mu\). Just as the slope \(p = f'(x)\) above determines \(x\) only when \(f'' \neq 0\), this works (locally) only when the mass matrix, the matrix of second derivatives \[W_{\mu\nu} = \frac{\partial^2 L}{\partial \dot q^\mu\, \partial \dot q^\nu}\] is invertible. For the harmonic oscillator of Chapter 3, the mass matrix is just the mass, \(W = m \neq 0\), so there is no problem. If \(\det W = 0\), the Lagrangian is called singular.

A singular example: the spring pendulum

A simple physical example is a pendulum of length \(\ell\) hung from a massless pivot, which is itself held by two springs (Figure 4.3); see the video lecture [2] for example.

The coordinates are \((x, y, \theta)\), and only the bob, at \(X = x + \ell\sin\theta\), \(Y = y - \ell\cos\theta\), has mass, so \[L = \frac{1}{2} m \Bigl[ \bigl(\dot x + \ell\dot\theta\cos\theta\bigr)^2 + \bigl(\dot y + \ell\dot\theta\sin\theta\bigr)^2 \Bigr] - U(x, y, \theta).\] Here \(U\) is the potential energy of the two springs and of gravity. We do not bother writing it out, since it does not matter for showing the singularity: it depends only on the coordinates, not on the velocities. Differentiating twice with respect to the velocities \((\dot x, \dot y, \dot\theta)\) gives the mass matrix \[W = m \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & \ell\cos\theta \\ 0 & 1 & \ell\sin\theta \\ \ell\cos\theta & \ell\sin\theta & \ell^2 \end{pmatrix}.\] This matrix has rank \(2\): the third column is a linear combination of the first two (\(\ell\cos\theta\) times the first plus \(\ell\sin\theta\) times the second). So \(W\) is not invertible, and \(L\) is singular.

Since \(W\) is a \(3\times 3\) matrix of rank \(2\), the rank–nullity theorem says that its null space is one-dimensional: there is a non-zero direction that \(W\) sends to zero, namely \[n = (-\ell\cos\theta,\, -\ell\sin\theta,\, 1), \qquad W n = 0,\] and the null space consists of the velocities \((\dot x, \dot y, \dot\theta) = c\,n\), for any number \(c\). Such velocities produce no momentum at all, since physically they correspond to a motion that does not move the bob at all (Figure 4.4). Consequently, adding one of them to any velocity \(\dot q\) does not change the momenta, since \(W(\dot q + c\,n) = W\dot q\): there is a whole class of velocities, \(\dot q + c\,n\) for every \(c\), that yield the same momenta. Given the momenta, we cannot tell which of these velocities we started from, which is why the inversion \(p_\mu \mapsto \dot q^\mu\), and with it the Legendre transformation, fails.

In general, singular Lagrangians require a more involved procedure known as the Dirac–Bergmann algorithm, which the video lecture [2] also covers. For a worked example of the full algorithm, see Brown [3].

Removing the singularity

The singularity can be made to disappear by assigning a mass \(M\) to the rod, spread uniformly along its length. The Lagrangian then becomes \[L = \frac{1}{2} m \bigl( \dot X^2 + \dot Y^2 \bigr) + \frac{1}{2} M \Bigl[ \bigl(\dot x + \tfrac{\ell}{2}\dot\theta\cos\theta\bigr)^2 + \bigl(\dot y + \tfrac{\ell}{2}\dot\theta\sin\theta\bigr)^2 \Bigr] + \frac{1}{2}\,\frac{M\ell^2}{12}\,\dot\theta^2 - U.\] Its mass matrix is \[W = \begin{pmatrix} m + M & 0 & \bigl(m + \frac{M}{2}\bigr)\ell\cos\theta \\ 0 & m + M & \bigl(m + \frac{M}{2}\bigr)\ell\sin\theta \\ \bigl(m + \frac{M}{2}\bigr)\ell\cos\theta & \bigl(m + \frac{M}{2}\bigr)\ell\sin\theta & \bigl(m + \frac{M}{3}\bigr)\ell^2 \end{pmatrix},\] with determinant \[\det W = \frac{M\ell^2\,(m + M)(4m + M)}{12}.\] In the case \(M = 0\) we recover the earlier result.
