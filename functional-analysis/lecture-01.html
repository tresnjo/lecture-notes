1  Norms

Functional analysis extends linear algebra and calculus to infinite-dimensional vector spaces, typically spaces of functions. The first ingredient we need is a way of measuring the size of a vector.

Norms

NoteDefinition 1.1 (Convex set). [1, §2.1]

A subset \(K\) of a vector space \(V\) is convex if, together with any two of its points \(k_1, k_2\), it contains every point \[(1-t)k_1 + t k_2, \qquad t \in [0,1],\] on the segment between them.

In words, the line segment between any two points of \(K\) stays inside \(K\) (Figure 1.1).

NoteDefinition 1.2 (Norm and normed vector space). [1, Def. 2.1]

Let \(V\) be a vector space over \(\mathbb R\) or \(\mathbb C\). A function \(\|\cdot\| : V \to \mathbb R\) is a norm if, for all \(v, w \in V\) and all scalars \(\alpha\),

  • \(\|v\| \geq 0\), with equality only for \(v = 0\) (strict positivity);
  • \(\|\alpha v\| = |\alpha|\,\|v\|\) (homogeneity);
  • \(\|v + w\| \leq \|v\| + \|w\|\) (triangle inequality).

A vector space together with a norm, \((V, \|\cdot\|)\), is a normed vector space.

CautionExample 1.3 (Normed real vector spaces). [1, Ex. 2.2]

Each of the following is a normed real vector space.

  1. \(\mathbb R^d\) with the Euclidean norm \(\|v\|_2 = \bigl(|v_1|^2 + \cdots + |v_d|^2\bigr)^{1/2}\).
  2. \(\mathbb R^d\) with the supremum norm \(\|v\|_\infty = \max_k |v_k|\).
  3. \(\mathbb R^d\) with \(\|v\|_1 = |v_1| + \cdots + |v_d|\).
  4. The space \(C_b(X)\) of bounded continuous functions \(f : X \to \mathbb R\) on a topological space \(X\), normed by the supremum \(\|f\|_\infty = \sup_{x \in X} |f(x)|\).

A good way to picture a norm is through its unit ball \(\{v : \|v\| \leq 1\}\). For the three norms on \(\mathbb R^2\) above these are a diamond, a disc and a square (Figure 1.2). The unit ball of a norm is always convex: if \(\|k_1\|, \|k_2\| \leq 1\), the triangle inequality and homogeneity give \(\|(1-t)k_1 + t k_2\| \leq (1-t) + t = 1\).

Normed spaces as metric and topological spaces

ImportantLemma 1.4 (Associated metric). [1, Lem. 2.4]

Let \((V, \|\cdot\|)\) be a normed vector space. For all \(v, w \in V\) the reverse triangle inequality \[\bigl|\|v\| - \|w\|\bigr| \leq \|v - w\| \tag{1.1}\] holds. The function \(d(v, w) = \|v - w\|\) is a metric on \(V\), and the norm \(V \to \mathbb R\) is continuous for the topology of this metric.

The lemma is a mouthful, so let us break it into its components. Equation (1.1) says that if \(\|v - w\| \to 0\), then also \(\bigl|\|v\| - \|w\|\bigr| \to 0\), which is exactly continuity of the norm. The second part says that every normed vector space is also a metric space.

ImportantProposition 1.5 (Every normed space is a metric space). [1, proof of Lem. 2.4]

Let \((V, \|\cdot\|)\) be a normed vector space. Then \(d(x, y) := \|x - y\|\) defines a metric on \(V\).

NoteProof

Non-negativity. \(d(x, y) = \|x - y\| \geq 0\) by positivity of the norm.

Definiteness. \(d(x, y) = 0 \iff \|x - y\| = 0 \iff x = y\).

Symmetry. \(d(x, y) = \|x - y\| = \|{-(y - x)}\| = \|y - x\| = d(y, x)\) by homogeneity.

Triangle inequality. \(d(x, z) = \|(x - y) + (y - z)\| \leq \|x - y\| + \|y - z\| = d(x, y) + d(y, z)\). \(\square\)

What does continuous with respect to the topology induced by \(d\) really mean? We first recall what a topology is.

NoteDefinition 1.6 (Topological space).

A topological space is a pair \((X, \mathcal O)\), where \(\mathcal O \subseteq \mathcal P(X)\) is a topology on \(X\), meaning

  1. \(\emptyset, X \in \mathcal O\);
  2. \(O, O' \in \mathcal O \Rightarrow O \cap O' \in \mathcal O\) (closed under finite intersections);
  3. \(\{O_i\}_{i \in I} \subseteq \mathcal O \Rightarrow \bigcup_{i \in I} O_i \in \mathcal O\) (closed under arbitrary unions).

The elements of \(\mathcal O\) are called open sets.

Given a metric \(d\) on a set \(X\), we induce a topology as follows.

NoteDefinition 1.7 (Metric topology).

Given a metric space \((X, d)\), the topology induced by \(d\) is \[\mathcal O_d = \{ U \subseteq X \mid \forall x \in U\ \exists \varepsilon > 0 : B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U \},\] where \(B_\varepsilon(x) = \{ y \in X \mid d(x, y) < \varepsilon \}\) is the open ball of radius \(\varepsilon\) centred at \(x\).

NoteProof that \(\mathcal O_d\) is a topology

\(\emptyset\) and \(X\). The condition is vacuous for \(\emptyset\), and every ball \(B_\varepsilon(x)\) lies in \(X\).

Finite intersections. Let \(U, U' \in \mathcal O_d\) and \(x \in U \cap U'\). There are \(\varepsilon, \varepsilon' > 0\) with \(B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U\) and \(B_{\varepsilon'}(x) \subseteq U'\). With \(\delta = \min(\varepsilon, \varepsilon')\) we get \(B_\delta(x) \subseteq U \cap U'\), so \(U \cap U' \in \mathcal O_d\).

Arbitrary unions. Let \(\{U_i\}_{i \in I} \subseteq \mathcal O_d\) and \(x \in \bigcup_i U_i\). Then \(x \in U_j\) for some \(j\), and some ball \(B_\varepsilon(x) \subseteq U_j \subseteq \bigcup_i U_i\). Hence \(\bigcup_i U_i \in \mathcal O_d\). \(\square\)

So a set is open when every one of its points has a small ball around it that stays inside the set (Figure 1.3). A normed vector space always has an associated metric, and hence always an induced topology.

NoteDefinition 1.8 (Continuous map).

A map \(f : X \to Y\) between topological spaces \((X, \mathcal O_X)\) and \((Y, \mathcal O_Y)\) is continuous if preimages of open sets are open: \[U \in \mathcal O_Y \implies f^{-1}(U) \in \mathcal O_X.\]

This is the same idea as for measurable functions in integration theory, where preimages of measurable sets are required to be measurable. There the structure on the sets is a \(\sigma\)-algebra; here it is a topology instead.

In Lemma 1.4 we have \(f = \|\cdot\| : V \to \mathbb R\), where \(V\) carries the topology \(\mathcal O_d\) induced by \(d(v, w) = \|v - w\|\), and \(\mathbb R\) carries the standard topology \(\mathcal O_{\text{std}}\) induced by the absolute value \(|\cdot|\). The second part of the lemma asserts that \[\forall\, U \in \mathcal O_{\text{std}}: \quad \|\cdot\|^{-1}(U) \in \mathcal O_d.\]

Equivalence of norms

Different norms may in principle produce different topologies. The strictest way to compare two norms would be to demand \(\|v\| = \|v\|'\) for all \(v \in V\). We relax this and instead compare norms through the topologies they induce.

ImportantLemma 1.9 (Equivalence of norms). [1, Lem. 2.5]

Two norms \(\|\cdot\|\) and \(\|\cdot\|'\) on \(V\) give the same topology exactly when they agree up to a constant factor: there is a \(c \geq 1\) with \[\frac1c \|v\|' \leq \|v\| \leq c \|v\|' \qquad \text{for every } v \in V.\] Such norms are called equivalent.

CautionExample 1.10 (Equivalent norms on \(\mathbb R^d\)).
  • \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) and \(\|\cdot\|_2\), with \(c = \sqrt d\): \(\ \|v\|_\infty \leq \|v\|_2 \leq \sqrt d\, \|v\|_\infty\).
  • \(\|\cdot\|_2\) and \(\|\cdot\|_1\), with \(c = \sqrt d\): \(\ \|v\|_2 \leq \|v\|_1 \leq \sqrt d\, \|v\|_2\).
  • \(\|\cdot\|_\infty\) and \(\|\cdot\|_1\), with \(c = d\): \(\ \|v\|_\infty \leq \|v\|_1 \leq d\, \|v\|_\infty\).

Geometrically, equivalence means that a scaled copy of each unit ball fits inside the other; in Figure 1.2 the square fits in the disc after shrinking by \(\sqrt 2\), and the disc fits in the square as it is. Every ball for one norm therefore contains a ball for the other around the same point, so both norms have the same open sets.

ImportantProposition 1.11 (Equivalence in finite dimensions). [1, Prop. 2.6]

For every \(d \geq 1\), all norms on \(\mathbb R^d\) are equivalent to each other.

Since every finite-dimensional real (complex) vector space of dimension \(n\) is isomorphic to \(\mathbb R^n\) (\(\mathbb C^n\)), see Theorem 1.12, any two norms on a finite-dimensional space induce the same topology. On infinite-dimensional spaces, different norms generally produce different topologies.

ImportantTheorem 1.12 (Finite-dimensional vector spaces are isomorphic to \(\mathbb R^n\)).

Let \(V\) be a finite-dimensional vector space over \(\mathbb R\) with \(\dim V = n\). Then \(V \cong \mathbb R^n\).

NoteProof

Since \(\dim V = n\), there exists a basis \(\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}\) of \(V\). Define \[T : \mathbb R^n \to V, \qquad T(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n) = \sum_{k=1}^n \alpha_k e_k.\]

Linearity. For \(x = (\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)\), \(y = (\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_n) \in \mathbb R^n\) and \(\lambda \in \mathbb R\), \[T(x + \lambda y) = \sum_{k=1}^n (\alpha_k + \lambda \beta_k) e_k = T(x) + \lambda T(y).\]

Surjectivity. Since \(\{e_1, \ldots, e_n\}\) spans \(V\), every \(v \in V\) can be written \(v = \sum_k \alpha_k e_k = T(\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n)\).

Injectivity. For a linear map, \(T(x) = T(y) \Rightarrow x = y\) amounts to \(T(x) = 0 \Rightarrow x = 0\). If \(T(x) = \sum_k \alpha_k e_k = 0\), linear independence of the basis gives \(\alpha_k = 0\) for all \(k\), so \(\ker T = \{0\}\).

Hence \(T\) is a bijective linear map, i.e. a linear isomorphism, and \(V \cong \mathbb R^n\). \(\square\)

Semi-norms and the quotient norm

NoteDefinition 1.13 (Semi-norm). [1, Def. 2.11]

A semi-norm (or pseudo-norm) on a vector space \(V\) is a function \(\|\cdot\| : V \to \mathbb R_{\geq 0}\) that is homogeneous and satisfies the triangle inequality, but may vanish on non-zero vectors.

Unlike a norm, a semi-norm does not demand \(\|v\| = 0 \Rightarrow v = 0\). Non-zero vectors can have zero semi-norm, and so two distinct points can have zero distance from each other.

TipRemark 1.14 (The topology induced by a semi-norm). [1, §2.1.2]

Defining \(d(v, w) = \|v - w\|\) as in Lemma 1.4 now gives a pseudo-metric, which can assign distance zero to distinct points, and Definition 1.7 still turns it into a topology on \(V\). This topology need not be Hausdorff (Definition 1.15 below).

NoteDefinition 1.15 (Hausdorff space).

A topological space \((X, \mathcal O)\) is Hausdorff if for all \(x \neq y\) in \(X\) there exist open sets \(U_x, U_y \in \mathcal O\) with \(x \in U_x\), \(y \in U_y\) and \(U_x \cap U_y = \emptyset\).

In other words, any two distinct points can be separated by neighbourhoods that do not intersect (Figure 1.4). The standard topology on \(\mathbb R\) is Hausdorff.

CautionExample 1.16 (A non-Hausdorff space).

Let \(X = \mathbb R\) and \[\mathcal O = \{\emptyset, \mathbb R\} \cup \{(a, \infty) \mid a \in \mathbb R\}.\] This is a topology, but it is not Hausdorff: for \(x < y\), every open set containing \(x\) is of the form \((a, \infty)\) with \(a < x\) (or \(\mathbb R\)), and so also contains \(y\).

For a semi-norm, the induced topology is Hausdorff precisely when the semi-norm is a norm. Indeed, a vector \(v \neq 0\) with \(\|v\| = 0\) lies in every ball \(B_\varepsilon(0)\), so \(v\) and \(0\) cannot be separated.

CautionExample 1.17 (Promoting the \(L^1\) semi-norm to a norm). [1, Ex. 2.12]

For a measure space \((X, \mathcal B, \mu)\), let \(L^1_\mu(X)\) be the space of measurable functions \(f : X \to \mathbb R\) that are integrable with respect to \(\mu\) (see Lecture 6 of the integration theory notes). Here the elements are actual functions, not equivalence classes. The integral \[\|f\|_1 = \int_X |f| \, d\mu\] is a semi-norm on this space, but in general not a norm, since \(\|f\|_1 = 0\) only forces \(f = 0\) \(\mu\)-a.e. Identifying functions that agree \(\mu\)-a.e., via \(f \sim g \Leftrightarrow f = g\) \(\mu\)-a.e., turns it into a norm. This is the quotient construction of Lemma 1.19 with \(W = V_0\), the functions that vanish a.e.

ImportantLemma 1.18 (Kernel of a semi-norm). [1, Lem. 2.14]

For a semi-norm on \(V\), the vectors of zero semi-norm, \(V_0 = \{v \in V \mid \|v\| = 0\}\), form a linear subspace, and this subspace is closed in the topology the semi-norm induces.

\(V_0\) is a linear subspace (as is easily verified), and it is closed since it is the preimage of the closed set \(\{0\}\)1 under the continuous map \(v \mapsto \|v\|\) (see Lemma 1.4).

1 Closed in the standard topology \(\mathcal O_{\text{std}}\) on \(\mathbb R\), which we assume unless otherwise stated: its complement \((-\infty, 0) \cup (0, \infty)\) is open. This depends on the topology; in the discrete topology, where every subset is open, every set is also closed.

ImportantLemma 1.19 (Quotient norm). [1, Lem. 2.15]

Let \(\|\cdot\|\) be a semi-norm on \(V\) and \(W \subseteq V\) a closed subspace. On the quotient space \(V/W = \{v + W \mid v \in V\}\), \[\|v + W\|_{V/W} = \inf_{w \in W} \|v + w\|\] is a norm. When \(W = V_0\) is the kernel of the semi-norm (closed by Lemma 1.18), this norm is simply \(\|v + V_0\|_{V/V_0} = \|v\|\).

The quotient norm finds the smallest norm among all representatives of the coset \(v + W\). Geometrically, it is the size of the smallest ball around \(0\) that touches the coset (Figure 1.5). For \(W = V_0\), every representative has the same semi-norm, since \(\|v\| - \|w\| \leq \|v + w\| \leq \|v\| + \|w\|\) with \(\|w\| = 0\).

Strict sub-additivity

NoteDefinition 1.20 (Strictly sub-additive norm). [1, Def. 2.17]

A norm on \(V\) is strictly sub-additive if equality in the triangle inequality only occurs for vectors pointing in the same direction: \[\|v + w\| < \|v\| + \|w\|\] for all \(v, w \in V\), except when \(v\) and \(w\) are both non-negative real multiples of \(v + w\).

CautionExample 1.21 (Strictly sub-additive norms on \(\mathbb R^n\)).

Strictly sub-additive: \((\mathbb R^n, \|\cdot\|_p)\) for \(1 < p < \infty\), since Minkowski’s inequality is strict unless the vectors are proportional.

Not strictly sub-additive:

  • \((\mathbb R^2, \|\cdot\|_1)\): \(v = (1, 0)\) and \(w = (0, 1)\) are not proportional, yet \(\|v + w\|_1 = 2 = \|v\|_1 + \|w\|_1\).
  • \((\mathbb R^2, \|\cdot\|_\infty)\): \(v = (1, 1)\) and \(w = (1, 0)\) are not proportional, yet \(\|v + w\|_\infty = 2 = \|v\|_\infty + \|w\|_\infty\) (compare [1, Ex. 2.19]).
